01 · Summary

The proposal in one page

We ask to be recognised as MicroAire's exclusive distributor in Colombia for the PAL line, and for four forms of support that convert a flat market into a growing one.

What we ask for. A certified service station in Colombia. Payment terms of 180 days, so that ACT can carry the credit its customers need. A structured surgeon training programme based in Bogotá. Support for marketing and congress activity: speakers at the meetings where ACT already builds and staffs the stand.

What we commit to. USD 526,000 in the coming year with none of the above. That is the floor, and it matches what was purchased last year. With the four requests in place, USD 604,900, which is the floor plus 15%, then 13% in year two and 8% a year after that.

What it is worth to you. Held across a five year agreement, the difference between those two paths is approximately USD 1.05 million in incremental revenue to you. That figure, not our enthusiasm, is the argument.

The one sentence that matters. Every surgeon relationship MicroAire has in Colombia today runs through one person, and that person is the owner of ACT. This proposal does not move the channel. It formalises where the channel already is.

And the part that is easiest to underestimate from Charlottesville: in this market trust is personal, and it is already ours. A surgeon here does not buy a PAL system from a catalogue. He buys it from someone who has stood beside him in theatre, who answers the phone at ten at night, and who was there the last time something went wrong. That is built one conversation at a time and it takes years.

This is what global brands mean by glocalisation, and it is the piece MicroAire has not yet looked at closely in this market. Product travels. Trust does not, unless somebody local carries it. Every request in this document is downstream of that one fact.

03 · Service

Where the brand is losing customers: service

Before anything we are asking for, this is the item that is costing MicroAire the most in Colombia, and it is invisible from Charlottesville.

A PAL handpiece is a mechanical instrument and it wears. With no certified maintenance in the country, a unit needing repair leaves Colombia and comes back about six weeks later. That much is a logistics problem. What follows is not.

Read that as a share of what the surgeon paid for the system, because that is how he reads it. Every year he spends more than a sixth of the value of the equipment just to keep it working, when the figure for a properly supported capital instrument sits at 5%.

And so surgeons stop servicing it. They wait five or six years instead of maintaining annually. By then the accumulated maintenance is close to 95% of the price of a new machine. When a unit does fail, the emergency repair takes long enough that the equipment cannot be held in regulatory compliance in the meantime.

The conclusion a Colombian surgeon reaches is not that MicroAire is expensive. It is that MicroAire is not viable to own. That sentence is being said in this market right now, and it is the single most damaging thing happening to the brand here.

The chain that follows is the part that costs you money. Service quality falls, so the equipment underperforms. The surgeon's theatre schedule gets blocked when a unit is away. His operating costs rise, because a machine that is not maintained wears faster and fails harder. And at the end of that chain the patient and the surgeon are both left with a poor impression of MicroAire, not of the distributor. The brand absorbs the damage from a problem that is entirely fixable at the service layer.

What we propose to do about it

Building our own certified station is not the fast answer. A new INVIMA authorisation for a repair station takes roughly two years. So we went looking for the alternative instead.

ACT has done the work of finding, comparing and filtering the options, and Novatécnica is the one that came out. They are already authorised and already service equipment of this class. The arrangement is not yet signed, and we are telling you that plainly rather than presenting it as done. What we are proposing is that it becomes a direct arrangement with ACT, so the relationship, the response time and the accountability sit in one place instead of leaving the surgeon between two companies.

What this is worth to MicroAire. It removes international repair shipping from your cost of serving Colombia, permanently. And it removes the reason surgeons currently give for not buying the next machine, which is a revenue problem dressed as a service problem.

The wider point, said respectfully

The current model requires every piece of equipment to travel to the United States to be repaired. Inside the United States that is efficient and sensible. Outside it, it is not a service model at all: it is a delay with paperwork attached. A brand of MicroAire's size eventually has to decide whether its service follows its sales across borders.

You have already started down this road in Europe, Mexico and Brazil. We are not claiming to be first, and we would not want to be: it is far better for us that the model already exists. What we are offering is to be the first one that works end to end, with a service arrangement, a training calendar and a commercial plan running together rather than separately. Colombia is small enough to prove it and large enough for the proof to mean something.

PAL competes on two fronts here. Against other power assisted systems sold in Colombia today, and against different modalities altogether, ultrasound and laser platforms. Where the all-in-one trend helps us, and it is worth naming. The industry is moving towards single closed platforms that do everything and are priced accordingly. Apyx is the clearest example: buying into AYON means buying the whole system at once, at platform prices.

MicroAire is sold in pieces, and that is a commercial advantage rather than a gap. A surgeon can buy a handpiece. He can buy a console later. He can add infiltration when his volume justifies it, and keep the cannulas he already trusts. We do sell the complete system and we prefer to, but a surgeon who cannot commit to a full platform this year is not lost to us the way he is lost to a closed system. In a market where capital is expensive, being modular is the reason a deal closes at all.

The uncomfortable part, and it is not about the product. MicroAire is losing ground on the two things this document has already described: what it costs to keep the equipment running, and how long it takes to get it fixed. Neither is a manufacturing problem. Both sit in the layer a manufacturer delegates to its distributor, and in Colombia that layer has not been worked.

No strategy has been deployed against either front. There has been no service plan, no maintenance offer the surgeon can accept, no answer to the six week wait. ACT has repeatedly proposed and pushed to address exactly these fronts, and has not been given the room to execute them. We are not raising this to assign blame. We are raising it because the gap is not a mystery to be solved: the work has been identified, offered and left undone, and the fix requires a distributor who is allowed to do it.

Where the brand actually stands

We will not soften this. In Colombia the reputation of the brand has fallen, and it has fallen for reasons that are fixable rather than fundamental. Surgeons who own the equipment talk to surgeons who are considering it, and what they pass on is the maintenance bill and the six week wait. Every one of the four requests in this proposal exists to reverse a specific part of that.

And there is an argument for doing it properly here. If a market where the brand has slipped can be turned around with a service arrangement, structured training and real commercial support, that is worth more than a market that was never in trouble. Recovery is the harder proof, and it is the one that transfers. Colombia can be the case study instead of the cautionary tale.

06 · This year

Why this year was flat, and what it tells us

This needs explaining precisely rather than left to sit. It was not price, and it was not the exchange rate.

The Colombian peso appreciated sharply. The representative market rate moved from COP 4,040.85 in July 2025 to COP 3,361.62 in July 2026, a fall in the dollar of about 17%. In peso terms your equipment became cheaper here, not more expensive.

The surgeon did not experience it that way, and that is the part that matters. He watched the dollar weaken all year and saw no movement in what he was asked to pay. ACT has wanted to move prices with the rate for some time and has not been able to: the list has stayed where it was. From where the surgeon sits, that reads as a brand taking the currency gain and keeping it, at exactly the moment he was deciding whether to invest.

Two things changed, and neither was price.

Money became expensive. Banco de la República raised its policy rate from 9.25% at the start of 2026 to 12.00% in June, a rise of 275 basis points in a single year, and held it there on 5 August. Annual inflation stood at 5.8% in May.

And the country spent the year waiting. The presidential contest was not settled until the run off of 21 June, and it was decided by under one point, 49.66% against 48.70%, a margin of roughly 250,800 votes. The new government took office on 7 August 2026. Through almost the entire commercial year, capital sat still. In a market where a large share of aesthetic patients come from abroad and their money stays in offshore accounts until the picture is clear, that is not background noise. It is the difference between a surgeon committing to a console this quarter or waiting to see who won.

What we are doing about it, which is better than cutting the price

Dropping prices to chase the rate would be the obvious move and the wrong one. It is hard to undo, it trains the market to wait for the next drop, and it hands margin away permanently in exchange for a currency swing that will reverse. So we are answering it through the commercial plan instead.

We package. The price stays where it is, and what the surgeon receives for it grows. A cannula has a working life of roughly 20 procedures, and in practice surgeons run them well past that. Not because replacements are slow, they arrive quickly, but because they are expensive, and a cannula that still cuts is hard to justify replacing. So the package includes a set of cannulas of his own choosing, together with items that are bought rarely and therefore never rotate.

This does three things at once, and only one of them is for the surgeon. It protects both of us from exchange rate volatility without ever touching the list price. It moves inventory that currently sits still, which improves rotation on your side and ours. And it raises the real average ticket per surgeon rather than discounting it, so the relationship gets deeper instead of cheaper. The exact composition of each package is still being defined.

So the constraint was never price. The equipment became more affordable in pesos while the cost of money rose and the country held its breath. Commercial terms are what let a distributor keep selling through a year like that, and MicroAire does not currently offer them. That is why the request for 180 day terms is not a concession we are seeking. It is the mechanism that keeps the channel moving when conditions do what they did this year.

07 · The requests

The four requests

Each is tied to a mechanism rather than to goodwill, and each has a stated effect on the number in section 08.

A certified service station in Colombia
Repairs in three to five days rather than six weeks. Removes the most frequently cited reason surgeons decline to renew, and removes international repair shipping from your costs.
Payment terms of 180 days
This is the request that unlocks the others, and it is not about ACT's cash flow. It is about the surgeon's.

The policy rate in Colombia moved from 9.25% to 12.00% inside a single year. At that cost of money a surgeon does not decline the equipment, he defers it, and a deferral is indistinguishable from a lost sale until it is too late to do anything about it. The answer the market actually responds to is credit at the point of decision.

ACT can extend that credit. It cannot extend terms it has not been given. The 180 days are what let ACT carry the surgeon, and carrying the surgeon is what opens the segment that is not buying today. We know this is a longer term than usual, and we are not asking for it lightly.
A structured training programme
Based in Bogotá, with local and international faculty, plus sponsored sessions at universities with plastic surgery specialisations. One-to-one access converts better than congress footfall and it seeds the next generation of users. Concretely, what we are asking for is slots on the courses you already run, a speaker where you can place one, and support in putting demonstration units in front of surgeons who have never held the handpiece.
Speakers at the congresses you already attend
At SAPS and TDABS MicroAire funds the stand, and ACT mounts it, brands it and staffs it. That part works and we are not asking to change it. What is missing is speakers. Both meetings draw surgeons from Central America, the United States, Europe and Asia, so a surgeon presenting PAL from that platform reaches far beyond Colombia, and that is worth more to the brand than it is to us. We are asking MicroAire to fund speakers at the congresses where ACT is present.

We should be straightforward about sequencing: the university programme will not sell a system this year. It is there because the surgeons who buy in 2030 are in a lecture theatre in 2027, and because nobody else is talking to them about MicroAire.

Why the year one target is conservative rather than modest

It matters that none of these mechanisms exists today. Everything sold in Colombia this year came from direct selling alone. There is no replacement programme, no university programme, no financing offer.

Take the replacement programme as the clearest example. Against an installed base of 350 surgeons, ACT commits to running a formal renewal campaign: reaching the surgeons who already own MicroAire equipment and making the case for upgrading. It is deliberately built on a small share of that base rather than on what the base could theoretically absorb. We commit to what we can hold, not to what looks better in a slide.

That is the posture across this whole document. Commit modestly and deliver above it, instead of committing high and explaining the gap. The first builds a relationship. The second ends one.
